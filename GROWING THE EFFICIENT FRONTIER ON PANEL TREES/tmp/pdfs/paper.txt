ABSTRACT
We develop a new class of tree-based models (P-Trees) for analyzing (unbalanced) panel data 
using
 economically guided, global (instead of local) split criteria that guard against overfitting 
while preserving sparsity and interpretability. To generalize security sorting and better estimate 
the efficient frontier, we grow a P-Tree top-down to split the cross section of asset returns to 
construct test assets and re-cover the stochastic discount factor under the mean-variance efficient 
framework, visualizing (asymmetric)  nonlinear interactions among firm characteristics. When 
applied to U.S. equities, boosted (multi-factor) P-Trees significantly advance the efficient frontier 
relative to those constructed with established factors and common test assets. P-Tree test assets 
are diversified and exhibit significant unexplained alphas against benchmark models. The unified 
P-Tree factors outperform most known observable and latent  factor models in pricing cross-
sectional returns, delivering transparent and effective trading strategies. Beyond asset pricing, our 
framework offers a more interpretable and computationally efficient alternative to recent machine 
learning and AI models for analyzing panel data through goal-oriented, high-dimensional  
clustering.

1 Introduction
Estimating the mean-variance efﬁcient (MVE) frontier remains crucial in asset
pricing and investment management. However, attempting to reach the efﬁcient fron-
tier by estimating the tangency portfolio from the high-dimensional, unbalanced panel
of individual asset returns proves impracticable (Cochrane, 2009). Empirical asset
pricing studies address the challenge by considering a “diversiﬁed” set of assets as
basis portfolios and identifying a few common factors to achieve multi-factor portfo-
lio efﬁciency (Fama, 1996). These approaches for using left-hand-side test assets and
right-hand-side risk factors are statistically equivalent in terms of spanning the efﬁ-
cient frontier (Gibbons, Ross, and Shanken, 1989), because under the MVE framework
with zero alphas, the collective Sharpe ratios of test assets equal those of risk factors.
However, popular factor models hardly span the common test asset universe (e.g.,
Kozak et al., 2018; Daniel et al., 2020; Lopez-Lira and Roussanov, 2020), let alone the
ultimate efﬁcient frontier constructed by individual asset returns.1 The empirical liter-
ature mostly uses ad hoc characteristics-sorted portfolios as test assets (e.g.,Fama and
French, 1993, 1996), which are typically limited to univariate- and bivariate-sorted ap-
proaches, due to the difﬁculties of high-dimensional sorting (Cochrane, 2011). Even
with dependent sorting (e.g., Daniel et al., 1997), researchers leave out nonlinearity
and asymmetric interactions, instead focus on interactions uniformly applicable to all
assets. The inability to capture the efﬁcient frontier hampers the effectiveness of model
estimations and evaluations (Lewellen et al., 2010; Ang et al., 2020). The key to bridg-
ing the gap between the true efﬁcient frontier and tangency portfolios of assets or fac-
tor portfolios lies in systematically utilizing the high-dimensional ﬁrm characteristics
that contain rich information on the joint distribution of asset returns dynamics.2
To this end, we introduce the Panel Tree (P-Tree) framework, a new and versa-
tile model family that clusters panel observations to achieve given economic objec-

split criteria for goal-oriented search. When applied to the panel of individual asset re-
turns, P-Trees utilize high-dimensional ﬁrm characteristics under the MVE framework
to jointly generate diversiﬁed test assets and latent factors for recovering the stochas-

Speciﬁcally, P-Trees employ a top-down approach, splitting the cross section of
thousands of individual assets and grouping them into a small number of clusters
with similar characteristic values to form value-weighted portfolios. Guided by asset
pricing considerations, we grow P-Trees following global split criteria to construct di-
versiﬁed test assets and latent factor models, estimating the efﬁcient frontier. Accord-
ing to Hansen and Jagannathan (1991), the resulting SDF that maximizes the Sharpe
ratio by spanning the efﬁcient frontier also minimizes the HJ-distance for explaining
the cross section. Generated under the uniﬁed framework, the resulting leaf basis port-
folios provide empirical researchers with a diversiﬁed set of test assets for model eval-
uation. Furthermore, the decision trees have an intuitive layout, allowing visualized
economic interpretability and identifying a sparse set of useful ﬁrm characteristics that
interact to jointly generate test assets and latent factors.
Fundamentally, P-Tree is a greedy algorithm guided by economic goals for opti-
mal clustering based on ﬁrm characteristic values within a large clustering space. In
asset pricing, basis portfolios are created by grouping assets based on classiﬁcations
such as country or industry, or through unsupervised clustering based on the corre-
lation of returns or ﬁrm characteristics. By contrast, P-Trees generate characteristics-
managed leaf basis portfolios to maximize the collective Sharpe ratio of the resulting

Methodological innovations. Our emphasis on the asset pricing application effec-
tively demonstrates the key methodological innovations. First, off-the-shelf ML meth-
ods, including the famous Classiﬁcation and Regression Trees (CART, Breiman et al.,
1984), typically assume that observations are independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.), and not designed for extracting heterogeneous information from panel data.
Though naively ﬁtting CART, boosted trees, or random forests on characteristics-return
data shows positive performance (e.g., Gu et al., 2020), they ignore the panel data
structure. By contrast, P-Tree fully embraces it and incorporates vectorized leaf pa-
rameters tailored for multi-period observations, enabling a time-invariant tree struc-
ture for all periods. The time-invariant P-Tree allows for interpretability when build-
ing ML models on panel data. As P-Tree grows, its nodes evolve into vectorized values
of basis portfolio returns, with the P-Tree factor being their MVE portfolio.
Second, the standard tree-based models in ML, including CART, focus purely
on prediction. Furthermore, CART-based models grow recursively, optimizing the
quadratic loss of prediction. These models optimize locally at each node without con-
sidering sibling nodes, mainly for computational efﬁciency. However, this “myopic”
strategy often leads to overﬁtting, because it operates on fewer observations in each
node as the tree grows. In contrast, P-Trees broaden the applications beyond pre-
diction, encompassing the generation of test assets and latent factors and explaining
cross-sectional returns. With an economics-guided optimization objective, the iter-
ative growth of P-Trees is designed to utilize data from the entire cross section to
guard against overﬁtting. P-Tree uses a nonlinear ML approach to generate test as-
sets in asset pricing, maximizing their collective Sharpe ratio with global split criteria.

The approach combines economic principles and statistical properties while ensuring

general approach for creating diversiﬁed basis portfolios under the MVE framework.
Third, P-Tree seamlessly integrates with ML’s boosting strategy, enabling multi-
ple factors to demonstrate exceptional asset pricing and investment performance. As
detailed in section 2.3, boosted and multi-factor P-Trees expand the set of assets and
factors by exploiting the unspanned efﬁcient frontier under the MVE framework. The
multi-factor efﬁciency of our boosted P-Trees enjoys a natural block-diagonal covari-
ance structure in portfolio construction, achieved by grouping individual assets into
P-Tree leaf test assets, without estimating a large covariance matrix of thousands of
test assets. Furthermore, P-Trees can assess incremental information on benchmark
factor models, such as the Fama-French factors, for interactive and nonlinear model-
ing of additional characteristics. Overall, when boosted to generate multiple factors,
P-Trees offer an alternative to PCA methods, with greater interpretability and sparsity,
while capturing (asymmetric) interactions.5
Empirical ﬁndings. First, we apply P-Tree to monthly U.S. individual stock returns
from 1981 to 2020 using 61 ﬁrm characteristics. P-Trees signiﬁcantly advance the efﬁ-
cient frontier with high annualized Sharpe ratios, ranging from 6.37 for a single P-Tree
(with 10 test assets) to 15.63 for 20 boosted P-Trees (with 200 test assets). These num-
bers are substantially higher than those constructed by conventional basis portfolios
(hundreds of univariate- or bivariate-sorted portfolios) or commonly used factor mod-
els. These ﬁndings provide strong evidence of a signiﬁcant gap between the current
empirical literature and the potential limits of the efﬁcient frontier. Moreover, gener-
ated under the uniﬁed MVE framework, boosted P-Trees produce multi-factor models
that effectively price the cross-sectional returns and offer annualized Sharpe ratios
over 3 out-of-sample in the recent 20 years.

signiﬁcant challenge for alternative factor models. We identify many economically

Including a large set of test assets presents empirical challenges, such as large
portfolio estimation or weak factors (Giglio et al., 2022), leading to the development
of new methods for selecting test assets in multiple studies (e.g., Daniel et al., 2020;

security sorting to a collection of decision trees. The authors focus on pruning or
regularizing the tree bottom-up without specifying a split criterion for tree growth and
manually specify a small set of split candidates and a shallow depth for initial trees
(since enumerating all possible tree conﬁgurations to prune from is computationally
infeasible). P-Trees differ by growing top-down to provide a goal-oriented clustering
approach and efﬁciently scanning the large space of generalized sequential sorting on
high-dimensional characteristics, allowing for asymmetric interactions.

2 Panel Tree for Asset Pricing

Conventional tree-based models such as CART have multiple limitations, render-
ing them unsuitable for economic or ﬁnancial panel data and for generating test assets
or pricing kernels. First, they treat asset returns as i.i.d in the cross section or the
time series. Second, they focus on predictions using statistical local split criteria, such
as minimizing the sum of squared error objectives in the child nodes of a particular
split. Therefore, they do not incorporate panel structure or economic restrictions. As
the tree grows, the number of observations in each node decreases, and idiosyncratic
noise causes overﬁtting. Although ensemble methods such as random forests reduce
overﬁtting, they are less interpretable than single trees.
P-Tree solves both problems by (i) using a vectorized time series of leaf basis port-
folio and (ii) using economic objectives that consider all observations instead of just
those in a parent node to guide the tree growth. (i) allows us to extract panel data to es-
timate the efﬁcient frontier thoroughly. (ii) iteratively builds P-Trees using global split
criteria to include economic principles and prevent overﬁtting, extending the scope of
trees from pure prediction to generating test assets.

2.2 Growing a P-Tree
P-Tree partitions the universe of individual assets into non-overlapping leaf nodes
based on the values of ranked characteristics with a time-invariant structure over T
periods, with It assets in each period.The leaf basis portfolios are (value-weighted)
portfolios of stocks within each leaf node, and their time series of returns are saved in
a vectorized leaf parameter. P-Tree splits sequentially from the root node and gener-
ates one extra leaf basis portfolio after each additional split. Speciﬁcally, it produces
j + 1 leaf basis portfolios after the j-th split, reducing the dimension from thousands
of individual stocks to j + 1 portfolios. This concept of characteristics-managed leaf
basis portfolios generalizes the popular characteristics-sorted portfolios to allow for
asymmetric interactions of multiple characteristics.

2.3 Boosted P-Trees
Boosting is an ML technique that combines weak learners to form a strong learner
(Freund and Schapire, 1997; Rossi and Timmermann, 2015). Boosted P-Trees sequen-
tially grow a list of additive trees that augment previous trees under the MVE frame-
work, which helps further span the efﬁcient frontier with additional basis portfolios
and generate multi-factor models. Considering existing factors, the boosting proce-
dure seamlessly integrates into the P-Tree framework by creating additional P-Tree
factors to maximize the collective Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio. The boosting
procedure is outlined below.
1. The initial factor, denoted asf1,t, can be either generated by the ﬁrst single P-Tree
or benchmark factors selected by researchers (e.g., the market factor).
2. The second factor f2,t is generated by the second P-Tree to complement the ﬁrst
factor and maximize the squared Sharpe ratio. The second tree growth follows a
similar boosting split criterion:
L(˜ck,m) = µ′
FΣ−1
F µF, (6)
where F = [f1,t,f 2,t]. The MVE portfolio is generated from all the factors directly.
3. Repeat Step 2 to sequentially generate P factors ft = [f1,t,··· ,f P,t] until the stop-
ping criteria are triggered (e.g., one ﬁnds insigniﬁcant squared Sharpe ratio im-
provement Barillas and Shanken, 2017).
The boosted P-Tree utilizes a two-step process to produce either the MVE port-
folio or the SDF by incorporating multiple sets of leaf basis portfolios. Initially, the
tree-speciﬁc MVE portfolio of all leaf basis portfolios within each P-Tree is computed
to determine the current P-Tree factor. Subsequently, this factor is merged with all pre-
vious P-Tree factors or benchmarks to create an all-tree MVE portfolio for the multi-
factor P-Tree model. Boosted P-Trees indirectly impose regularization on the all-tree
MVE portfolio weights through the sequential tree block structure, even though the
15

=== PDF PAGE 17 ===
resulting Sharpe ratio may not surpass the one generated directly from all basis port-
folios in all P-Trees. This avoids high-dimensional estimation issues and, in turn, leads
to robust estimation and out-of-sample performance.
2.4 Simulation Evidence
Simulation studies in this section demonstrate the ability of P-Tree models to se-
lect characteristics and the effectiveness of P-Tree test assets in capturing non-linear
interactions among characteristics. In Appendix I, we calibrate the return-generating
process on the same empirical data sample, considering three true characteristics, their
interactions, and nonlinear terms. Additionally, we include a large set of redundant
characteristics that are correlated with the true characteristics but do not drive returns.
First, we compare the Sharpe ratios and alphas of P-Tree test assets with conven-
tional characteristics-sorted portfolios, which cannot model the nonlinear characteris-
tics interactions. The single P-Tree and boosted P-Trees consistently produce leaf basis
portfolios with higher Sharpe ratios and larger unexplained pricing errors under vari-
ous signal-to-noise-ratio levels, both in-sample and out-of-sample. Even though sort-
ing is performed on the true characteristics directly, univariate- and bivariate-sorted
portfolios still underperform P-Tree test assets generated on high-dimensional char-
acteristics. This result conﬁrms our empirical ﬁndings that P-Tree test assets achieve
better mean-variance efﬁciency than sorted portfolios as in Section 4.2.
Second, we demonstrate that P-Tree helps select the sparse true characteristics
and a large number of redundant or useless ones. The interpretability of the single
P-Tree relies on its ability to select variables. With repeated simulations, we show high
frequencies for P-Tree for selecting the true characteristics in the ﬁrst few splits. These
correct selection rates are higher for cases with a high signal-to-noise ratio. This ﬁnd-
ing can only be identiﬁed from simulations, because we remain agnostic regarding the
true sparse characteristics in the real-world data. The consistent selection properties
are related to evaluating characteristics’ importance in Section 5.1.
Finally, we evaluate the efﬁciency loss without observing all true characteristics
but still observe a large set of redundant or useless characteristics. This exercise eval-
uates the model’s performance with incomplete or useless information, which may
16

occur for various reasons. Under our calibrated return-generating process, the Sharpe
ratios and CAPM alphas decline substantially, and P-Tree selects redundant or useless
characteristics more frequently. Therefore, when a large set of characteristics is pro-
vided, we should include all of them rather than performing ad hoc variable selection
in empirical studies. For more details, please refer to Appendix I.

of portfolio returns from a time-invariant P-Tree structure. These leaf basis portfolios
are nonlinear, interactive, and high-dimensional characteristics-managed portfolios.

Advantages of P-Tree clustering. P-Tree has two advantages that make these test as-
sets efﬁcient. First, P-Tree is a goal-oriented clustering algorithm tailored for spanning
the efﬁcient frontier iteratively. Each leaf clusters individual asset returns based on
similar characteristic values, aggregating as a time series of portfolio returns. The goal
is to maximize the collective Sharpe ratio of the MVE portfolio spanned by the leaf ba-
sis portfolios. The economic objective of P-Tree is vastly different from the CART and
other off-the-shelf ML models, which focus on statistical return prediction without
economic utilities, and cannot ever tackle the task of growing efﬁcient frontier.
Second, P-Tree exploits the complex asymmetric interactions and nonlinearities
on high-dimensional characteristics. The commonly used test assets are characteristics-
sorted portfolios, such as univariate- and bivariate-sorted portfolios, which only con-
sider decile or quintile sorting, along with up to three characteristics chosen ad hoc
or based on researchers’ domain expertise. In contrast, P-Tree is more ﬂexible, al-
lowing for general sequential and simultaneous sorting with more characteristics and
asymmetric interactions in a uniﬁed framework. P-Trees enable the inclusion of large
leaves, like N15, as shown in Figure 4, where further splitting does not result in a
higher Sharpe ratio, as well as small leaves, such as N8. The asymmetric splitting
allows P-Trees to focus on the ﬁner clusters of informative assets while paying less
attention to non-informative assets under the MVE framework.

4.4 Investing P-Tree Factors
The P-Tree framework clusters thousands of assets into a number of leaf basis
portfolios, reducing portfolio optimization complexity. These P-Tree test assets can be
used as factors directly or as building blocks toward MVE portfolios for investment.
Additionally, the frequency of rebalancing P-Tree investment strategies can be reduced
to quarterly or annually to decrease transaction costs.
Full-sample evaluation. We evaluate the investment performance of P-Tree factors by

5.1 Characteristic Importance via Random P-Forest
First, our simulation evidence in Appendix I demonstrates the efﬁciency loss re-
sulting from missing true characteristics and illustrates how the nonlinear and interac-
tive P-Tree can recover the true set of characteristics, despite a large number of redun-
dant or useless characteristics. Second, the conventional sequential or independent
asset sorting scheme primarily focuses on no more than three characteristics in prac-
tice, due to the high-dimensional challenge. Because enumerating all possible sequen-
tial sorting cases with multiple characteristics is NP-hard, whether using tree models
or not, P-Tree’s iterative growth is an efﬁcient algorithm for overcoming this problem
and make computation feasible. Therefore, the characteristics selected by the P-Tree
algorithm are better proxies for the true fundamental risk inherent in the SDF, which
may be overlooked in a linear factor model with ad hoc selected factors.
Random P-Forest. We adopt another famous ensembling scheme in ML to evaluate
characteristic importance and account for variable selection risk. Random Forest ﬁts
multiple trees to avoid overﬁtting and quantifying model uncertainty.20 We utilize this
approach to assess the signiﬁcance of variables within the P-Tree framework. In the
subsequent text, we refer to our approach as Random P-Forest.
The ensemble steps for constructing the Random P-Forest are as follows: (i) boot-
strap the data on the time-series horizon with replacement, preserving the complete
cross section of the panel data for the selected time periods to exploit the low serial
correlations among asset returns. (ii) randomly draw 20 characteristics, and P-Trees
grow on each bootstrap sample independently. We repeat the procedure 1,000 times
to form a forest of 1,000 P-Trees. (iii) evaluate the importance of each characteristic by
counting its appearance in the P-Forest.
Our empirical exercises focus on the frequency of selecting one variable as a split

quantiﬁcation of the uncertainty of model estimation.

5.2 Evaluating a Characteristic with P-Tree.
As an alternative pricing kernel, P-Tree can directly evaluate a new characteris-
tic’s incremental (and nonlinear) contribution over a benchmark to complement the
literature. Researchers create a new factor by sorting based on a speciﬁc characteristic
in empirical studies. A crucial research task is to test the independent information of
new factors by controlling benchmark models through spanning regression (Fama and
French, 1996). However, due to the three-dimensional data structure of ﬁrm character-
istics, empirical studies rarely examine whether the associated characteristics provide
38

=== PDF PAGE 40 ===
new information and may suffer information loss (Lettau,2023). To address this issue,
P-Tree provides a new perspective for evaluating the usefulness of characteristics in
generating test assets and latent factor models, given a set of benchmark characteris-
tics, such as ME and BM.
In Figure 8, we demonstrate two baseline P-Trees: a three-layer ME baseline P-
Tree where all splits are based on ME and a 3-layer ME-BM baseline P-Tree where the
ﬁrst layer is split on ME and the second layer is split on BM. To evaluate the incremen-
tal utility for estimating the efﬁcient frontier of a characteristic against ME, we expand
the ME baseline P-Tree with one more split on the characteristic of interest or ME. One
might expect a split to increase the Sharpe ratio of an MVE portfolio for a shallow
P-Tree. Therefore, we only determine if a characteristic provides new information, by
comparing the Sharpe ratio improvement of adding this characteristic to the baseline
P-Tree over a split on ME. This approach allows us to measure the marginal contribu-
tion of the characteristic of interest.

6 Conclusion
Estimating the mean-variance efﬁcient (MVE) frontier and generating diversiﬁed
test assets using individual asset returns remain long-term challenges in empirical as-
set pricing (e.g., Markowitz, 1952; Lewellen et al., 2010; Daniel et al., 2020). Our paper
presents a novel interpretable AI model, Panel Tree (P-Tree), that effectively addresses
the traditional empirical challenge of generalized security sorting and delivers supe-
rior performance for analyzing the (unbalanced) panel of individual asset returns. P-
Tree utilizes high-dimensional characteristics containing rich information on the joint
distribution of asset returns, to generate basis portfolios as diversiﬁed test assets and
building blocks of the pricing kernel. More generally, P-Trees broaden tree-based mod-
els beyond pure prediction and analyze panel data effectively with economics-guided
tree growth, while retaining interpretability and handling asymmetric and nonlinear
interactions in noisy environments.
Our empirical study of U.S. equities shows that the efﬁcient frontiers spanned by
P-Tree test assets vastly outperform those from commonly used observable and ma-
43

=== PDF PAGE 45 ===
chine learning latent factor models, as well as conventional test assets. Boosted P-Trees
generate sequences of test assets to challenge all existing factor models, providing a
diversiﬁed test asset alternative to characteristic-sorted portfolios. In addition to leaf
basis portfolios, our uniﬁed approach under the MVE framework provides tradable
P-Tree factors and out-of-sample annualized Sharpe ratios over 3. The factor models
also outperform benchmark models in explaining the cross-sectional return variation.
Finally, P-Tree offers a new nonlinear and interactive framework to assess the con-
ditional information of high-dimensional characteristics, complementing traditional
factor-spanning regression in evaluating ﬁrm characteristics.

I Simulation
In this section, simulation studies are conducted to demonstrate the practical ca-
pability of P-Tree in choosing characteristics and the efﬁciency of P-Tree test assets in
capturing non-linear interactions among characteristics. The positive simulation ev-

return-generating process using the same empirical data sample and consider three
genuine characteristics and their interactions and nonlinear terms without loss of gen-
erality. Moreover, to simulate the true investor data environment, we consider a large
set of useless or noisy characteristics that correlate with the true characteristics but do
not impact generating returns.

be selected in scenarios of low signal-to-noise ratios or small sample sizes. The true
return-generating process is assumed only to involve three characteristics among all

III.D Time-Series Split
The panel data of asset returns has cross-sectional and time-series dimensions.
Most literature focuses on explaining the cross-sectional return variation, as do the
baseline P-Tree models, without considering potential structural breaks or regime shifts.
Because the tree grows from top to bottom, the panel of assets is partitioned into many
leaf nodes based on past characteristics. Each leaf node maintains the entire time se-
ries from period 1 toT but a subset of assets. However, empirical ﬁndings suggest that
factor choices, risk premia, and betas can be time-varying under different macroeco-
nomic states. For example, extensive literature examines regime changes in ﬁnancial
markets involving periods of high and low volatility or inﬂation, booms, or recessions
(e.g., Hamilton and Lin, 1996; Buraschi and Jiltsov, 2005).
With its ability to split nodes in the time-series dimension, P-Tree provides an al-
ternative approach to constructing and estimating factor-beta with regime-switching.
Moreover, the time-series information extracted from macro variables proves help-
ful in constructing the dynamic SDF. By splitting nodes (e.g., root node) by macro
variables instead of ﬁrm characteristics, we can create time-varying factor models
and estimate corresponding conditional betas under switching macroeconomic states.
Each child node maintains the same cross section of assets as the parent node, but
their time-series samples do not overlap. This approach improves P-Tree’s ﬂexibility
in capturing cross-sectional and time-series variations, providing clustering over the
two-dimensional panel.
